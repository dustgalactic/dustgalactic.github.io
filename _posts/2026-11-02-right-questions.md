Ever since I became a product manager, I was trying to catch up. The role date back many decades. When I first started, I was focusing solely on delivery, without much understanding what sets product management apart from project management. Faster, brighter, more!

It couldn't last for long, though. It was too exhausting.

At that point I started looking around at what my fellow product managers were doing. Am I lacking some certification, I wondered? Something about how to be EVEN FASTER at shipping EVEN MORE? Thankfully (for my budget — have you seen these course prices?), I reached out for advice. And I was given it, in a format of a long list of many books.

## Books? Books

As Baldur Bjarnason [points out in his essay](https://www.baldurbjarnason.com/2026/02-why-are-short-videos-bad-for-learning/), some formats are just better suited for learning. For me personally, books have always been the answer. I can't really focus on audio-information. I also have trouble difficulty focusing on written content in the digital format. Sounds ironic for a person who used to write tech docs, but I'd argue that's what made me good at it in the first place, as I never had the patience to bear through long, unstructured, or poorly written docs.

I was lucky to receive a good starting set. Some of the thing I didn't think I needed to dig into — why do I need to read a marketing book? How to formulate success metrics and measure what you build? How to talk to customers? Why do I need to talk to customers? I'm in the business of shipping features, you see... What do you say, I need to always talk to customers, forever and ever, so long as I'm a product manager?

Oh.

## The art of asking the right question

There's this paragraph in [Be Exceptional](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/55507035-be-exceptional) on the widely known Dunning-Kruger effect:
> It takes self-awareness and that higher component, self-mastery, to understand when we lack ability or expertise. In other words, the hallmark of good, intelligent decision-making is to "be good at knowing what we don't know" and likewise knowing what is not a good course to take—otherwise anything we decide to do seems okay.
> <div align="right">— Joe Navarro, Toni Sciarra Poynter<br/>Be Exceptional: Master the Five Traits That Set Extraordinary People Apart</div>
