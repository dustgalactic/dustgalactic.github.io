## The challenge

Technical products accumulate complexity quickly: expert language, edge cases, dependencies, and competing priorities all become part of the experience.

The challenge is to preserve what is powerful about the product without asking people to understand its entire internal world first.

## My approach

I begin by learning enough to ask useful questions. I speak with the people who
build the product and the people who use it, then map the moments where context
is missing or choices become difficult.

From there, I give the problem a structure:

1. **Find the decision behind the request.** A feature request often points to a
   deeper job that someone is trying to complete.
2. **Make the invisible visible.** Shared diagrams, plain language, and concrete
   examples help a team see assumptions and gaps early.
3. **Reduce before adding.** Clearer hierarchy and fewer choices can solve a
   problem more effectively than another layer of functionality.
4. **Test the story.** If the product direction cannot be explained simply, it
   usually needs another round of thinking.

<blockquote class="case-study-quote">
  <p>Clarity is not decoration added at the end. It is part of how the product is shaped.</p>
</blockquote>

## What this creates

This way of working gives teams a shared understanding of the problem and a
product direction that is easier to discuss, test, and refine. It also makes the
eventual experience more approachable for people who do not share the team’s
technical context.

My background in technical writing is central to the process. It trained me to
notice ambiguity, follow a user’s path through unfamiliar territory, and make
every piece of information earn its place.
